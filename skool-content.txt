TITLE:
Why Former Athletes Get Stuck (And How to Break the Pattern)

CONTENT:
------ copy everything below this line ------

This is the foundation everything else builds on.

Most former athletes don't struggle with motivation — they struggle with the gap between who they used to be and who they are now.

You know what hard work feels like. You've done it. You've pushed through difficult training, competed under pressure, experienced what your body is capable of. You have a reference point — and your current fitness doesn't match it.

That's not a motivation problem. That's a disconnect problem.

The gap between "who I was athletically" and "who I am right now" creates a psychological friction that makes it harder to train consistently, not easier.

When former athletes show up to a workout, they subconsciously measure themselves against their peak. A 35-year-old who played college soccer at 21 doesn't just feel tired after a hard run — they feel the distance between now and then. And that distance feels like failure, even when it's just... time.

So they either push too hard (trying to reclaim the past quickly) or stop altogether (because slow progress toward a past standard feels pointless). Both paths lead to the same place: stuck.


WHY CONVENTIONAL FITNESS ADVICE MAKES IT WORSE

Walk into any fitness community and you'll find advice built for one of two people: total beginners or elite athletes. The former athlete is neither.

You don't need "getting started" content. You know what a squat is. You've trained before.

But you also can't train like you did at 19 or 22 without consequences that 19-year-old you never experienced. Recovery takes longer. Overuse injuries are more likely. Life has real demands on your time.

Generic training plans assume a clean slate. Former athletes don't have clean slates — they have complex histories. Old injuries, ingrained movement patterns, deeply held expectations, and a complicated relationship with their own performance.

The advice doesn't fit the person. So the plan fails. And the former athlete concludes they're the problem.

They're not.


THE PATTERN (AND WHERE IT BREAKS)

The cycle looks like this:

Step 1: Motivation arrives — a race, a health scare, or just "enough is enough"
Step 2: You start training — often too hard, too fast, too much
Step 3: Something breaks down — injury, exhaustion, life
Step 4: You stop
Step 5: Months pass
Step 6: Back to Step 1

This can repeat for years. Each time, a little more confidence gets chipped away.

The break doesn't come from more motivation or a better plan (at least not at first). It comes from a shift in how you define being an athlete NOW — not in comparison to who you were then.

You are not rebuilding who you used to be. You are building who you are becoming.


THE THREE PHASES OF THE REBUILD

Phase 1 — Assessment: Understand where you actually are. Not where you used to be.

Phase 2 — Foundation: Build aerobic capacity, movement quality, sustainable habits. Slow and unsexy and absolutely essential.

Phase 3 — Performance: Layer in intensity and progression on top of a real foundation. This is where PRs happen.

Most people skip to Phase 3. That's why they get hurt and end up back at square one. We start at Phase 1.

TITLE:
Your Movement Baseline — The Assessment Every Athlete Needs Before Training

CONTENT:
------ copy everything below this line ------

The biggest mistake returning athletes make is starting a training program before they understand where they're actually starting from.

Before you build anything, you need honest data. This page walks you through it.


PART 1: AEROBIC FITNESS BASELINE

Option A — The Cooper Test (if you can run):
Find a flat route or track. Run as far as you can in 12 minutes. Record the distance.

Under 1.5 miles: developing aerobic base
1.5 to 1.8 miles: moderate aerobic fitness
1.8 to 2.0 miles: solid aerobic foundation
Over 2.0 miles: good aerobic capacity

Option B — The Talk Test:
Run at an easy effort. Can you speak in full sentences comfortably? That's your aerobic zone. If you can't, your base needs development before adding intensity.

Record your result. Don't judge it. This is day one data.


PART 2: MOVEMENT QUALITY ASSESSMENT

Run through all five:

1. Squat Pattern
Bodyweight squat, feet shoulder-width, toes slightly out. Squat as deep as comfortable with heels on the ground.
Watch for: heels rising, knees caving inward, severe forward lean — signals limited ankle or hip mobility.

2. Single-Leg Balance
Stand on one foot for 30 seconds, eyes open, then closed. Repeat on other side.
Watch for: significant wobble, hip drop, or inability to hold 30 seconds — signals hip stability weakness.

3. Hip Hinge
Hinge forward at the hip, pushing hips back until you feel your hamstrings engage. Not the lower back.
Watch for: rounding through the lower back — common and worth addressing before adding load.

4. Hip Flexor Length
Lunge position, one knee on floor, other foot forward. Squeeze the glute of the back leg and gently tuck pelvis.
Watch for: significant tightness — very common in people who sit for work, affects running stride directly.

5. Ankle Mobility
Stand 4-5 inches from a wall. Drive front knee toward the wall while keeping heel on floor. Can you touch?
Watch for: inability to touch, or significant side-to-side difference — affects running gait directly.


PART 3: LIFESTYLE BASELINE

Answer these honestly:

Sleep: How many hours of actual sleep per night?
Nutrition: Are you eating enough to support training? Eating within 45 min after workouts?
Hydration: Drinking water consistently throughout the day?
Current Activity: How many days per week are you moving intentionally right now?
Stress: On a scale of 1-10, what is your average daily stress?

Write actual answers. Your training prescription depends on all of them.


ACTION ITEM

Complete all three parts today. Write down your results and flag anything that stood out in the movement section. This is your baseline document — keep it. You'll reassess in four weeks.

TITLE:
How to Build Your First 4-Week Training Structure

CONTENT:
------ copy everything below this line ------

A training plan that doesn't fit your actual life isn't a plan — it's a fantasy.

In this page, you'll build a four-week structure grounded in your baseline, honest about your schedule, and designed to build the habit of consistency before it builds anything else.


THE PRINCIPLE BEFORE THE PLAN

In the first four weeks, consistency is the goal. Not fitness. Not speed. Not impressive sessions. Consistency.

Four solid weeks of moderate, consistent training produces real adaptation. One great week followed by two weeks of inconsistency produces almost nothing.

Your first block is not about what you can endure. It's about what you can sustain.


STEP 1: ESTABLISH YOUR WEEKLY TEMPLATE

Look at your actual schedule — not your ideal schedule. When are you realistically going to train?

For most returning athletes, 3-4 training days per week in this first block is right.

4-Day Template:
Day 1 (e.g. Monday): Easy aerobic run or walk/run — 20-35 min
Day 2 (e.g. Wednesday): Strength and mobility — 30-40 min
Day 3 (e.g. Friday): Easy aerobic run — 25-40 min
Day 4 (e.g. Saturday): Longer easy effort — 40-55 min

3-Day Option:
Day 1: Easy run — 20-30 min
Day 2: Strength and mobility — 30 min
Day 3: Longer easy run — 35-45 min

The specific days matter less than the pattern. Block the time.


STEP 2: DEFINE "EASY" FOR YOUR CURRENT FITNESS

Easy effort means you can speak in full sentences without laboring. If you're breathing too hard to hold a conversation, you're going too fast.

Walk/Run structure for lower baseline fitness:
Walk 2 min, jog 1 min. Repeat for 20-25 minutes. Shift progressively: 1:1 ratio, then 1:2, then a steady jog.

All sessions in weeks 1-4 should be easy. No intentional intensity in this block. Resist the urge to push pace.


STEP 3: INCLUDE STRENGTH AND MOBILITY WORK

Running without strength work is training with one hand tied behind your back.

Hip Stability (every strength day):
Glute bridge: 3 x 15
Clamshell: 3 x 15 per side
Single-leg glute bridge: 2 x 10 per side
Lateral band walk: 2 x 20 steps per direction

General Strength (twice per week):
Bodyweight squat: 3 x 15
Reverse lunge: 3 x 10 per side
Hip hinge: 3 x 12
Push-up: 3 x moderate fatigue

Mobility (daily if flagged in your assessment):
Kneeling hip flexor stretch: 2 x 60 sec per side
Wall ankle mobilization: 2 minutes


STEP 4: THE 4-WEEK PROGRESSION

Week 1: Conservative. Every session should finish with gas left in the tank. Build the pattern.
Week 2: Same schedule. Allow duration to increase slightly if it feels natural.
Week 3: You may feel cumulative fatigue. Don't increase load — that's the training working.
Week 4: Intentional reduction in volume. Shorter sessions, same schedule. This is your deload. Your body adapts during recovery. Week 4 is programming, not slacking.

By end of week 4, you'll have one month of consistent training. That is the foundation.


ACTION ITEM

Build your actual 4-week schedule today. Open your calendar. Block your training days for the next four weeks. Write out Week 1 with specific details — duration, type, effort level. The plan should be ready to execute tomorrow.

TITLE:
The Nutrition Framework That Supports Real Athletic Performance

CONTENT:
------ copy everything below this line ------

Note: I'm a running coach, not a registered dietitian. What's here is based on what's worked for me and the athletes I train — not medical advice. Talk to a qualified professional if you have specific health conditions or dietary needs.

Most nutrition advice for athletes is either too complex or too vague. This is a practical, flexible framework built for real people with real schedules.


THE FOUNDATION: FOOD IS FUEL

Food is information for your body. It signals hormones, fuels performance, supports recovery, and either enables or limits adaptation.

When you under-eat, over-restrict, or fuel inconsistently, your training suffers — regardless of how good the training itself is.

The goal of nutrition for athletes is not minimization. It's optimization. Eat enough. Fuel intentionally. See food as an investment in the work you're doing.


THE THREE NON-NEGOTIABLES

1. Eat enough total calories
The most commonly violated rule. If you're training 4+ hours per week and eating at a significant caloric deficit, you are impairing recovery, elevating injury risk, disrupting sleep, and limiting performance.

Rough starting estimate: body weight in pounds x 15-17 if training 4-6 hours per week. Adjust up for higher mileage.

2. Hit your protein target
Endurance athletes need 1.4-1.7 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight daily. For most people: 110-150 grams per day.

Protein is the building material for muscle repair, connective tissue, and immune function. Without it, you don't recover — you accumulate damage.

Distribute across meals. Aim for 30-40 grams per meal, 3-4 times per day.

3. Fuel around your training
Pre-training (sessions over 60 min): easily digestible carbs, 200-300 calories, low fat and fiber, 60-90 minutes before.

Post-training (within 45 minutes): 20-30g protein plus carbohydrates to begin glycogen replenishment.


THE MACRONUTRIENT BREAKDOWN

Carbohydrates — 45-55% of total daily calories
Your primary fuel source. Non-optional for endurance athletes.
Best sources: oats, rice, sweet potatoes, whole grain bread/pasta, fruit, legumes.

Protein — 25-30% of total daily calories
Repair, rebuild, perform.
Best sources: chicken, turkey, beef, salmon, eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, protein powder.

Fat — 20-30% of total daily calories
Hormonal health, satiety, long-duration energy.
Best sources: avocado, olive oil, nuts, fatty fish, eggs.

You don't need to track every gram every day. But understanding the balance helps you notice when something is off.


PRACTICAL MEAL STRUCTURE

Breakfast: High protein, moderate carbs.
Examples: eggs and toast and fruit / Greek yogurt and granola and berries / oatmeal and protein powder

Lunch: Your largest meal. Protein + complex carbs + vegetables + healthy fat.
Examples: grain bowl with chicken, rice, roasted veg, avocado / large salad with grilled protein and whole grain bread

Afternoon snack: Bridges the gap, prevents arriving at dinner depleted.
Examples: cottage cheese and fruit / apple and nut butter / protein shake and banana

Dinner: Moderate. Protein + vegetables + starch if training again within 12-16 hours.


HYDRATION

Drink water consistently throughout the day — not in large quantities all at once.
On training days over 60 minutes, add electrolytes.
Target: urine should be pale yellow.

If you're chronically dehydrated during the workday, set a reminder to drink water every 60-90 minutes. One habit closes most hydration gaps.


WHAT TO STOP WORRYING ABOUT

Eating perfectly — consistency and adequacy beat perfection every time
Exact timing to the minute — principles matter more than precision
Cutting out entire food groups without a clear reason
One difficult day — good weeks absorb hard days just fine

Eat well most of the time. Hit your protein. Fuel around training. That's it.


ACTION ITEM

For the next three days, write down everything you eat — not to judge it, but to see it clearly. At the end, look at: average daily protein, whether you ate before and after training, and whether total intake seems adequate. Identify one specific change to make this week.

POST TEXT:
The most common mistake I see from returning runners:

They add miles before they build capacity. They think more volume = faster progress. It doesn't.

More volume on a weak aerobic base = more injury, more burnout, slower development.

The first thing I work on with every athlete is easy aerobic development. Not pace. Not distance. The ability to run consistently at a truly easy effort — and have that feel manageable, not miserable.

DAY 3 — June 5 — Nutrition
POST TITLE: The Fueling Mistake That's Killing Your Training

POST TEXT:
The number one nutrition mistake I see in athletes who are training hard but not recovering well:

They're not eating enough.

Specifically — they're not eating enough after training. Post-workout nutrition is when your muscles are most primed to absorb protein and replenish glycogen. If you're skipping it or waiting 2-3 hours, you're leaving recovery on the table.

Simple fix: Within 45 minutes of finishing any training session, get in 20-30g of protein and some carbohydrates.

It doesn't need to be elaborate. A protein shake and a banana works. Greek yogurt and a piece of fruit works. Chicken and rice works.

The timing matters. Don't skip it.

DAY 7 — June 9 — Training
POST TITLE: The Long Run Isn't What You Think It Is

POST TEXT:
Most runners do their long run wrong.

They go too fast. They treat it like a race effort. They finish it proud of their pace and then spend the rest of the week more fatigued than they should be.

The long run is an aerobic development tool, not a fitness test.

The goal of a long run:
- Build time on feet
- Develop fat oxidation
- Train your body to sustain effort over distance
- Practice fueling and pacing

None of those goals require you to go fast. In fact, going fast actively works against most of them.

A properly executed long run should feel almost boring. Easy, conversational effort. If you're working hard, you're going too fast.

Slow your long runs down. Your recovery will improve, your aerobic base will develop faster, and you'll actually be able to train the next day.

DAY 8 — June 10 — Nutrition
POST TITLE: Protein: The One Thing Most Runners Consistently Under-Eat

POST TEXT:
Endurance athletes need more protein than most people think.

The research is consistent: 1.4-1.7 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day for endurance athletes. For a 180 lb athlete, that's roughly 115-140 grams per day.

Most people I talk to are getting 60-80g. That's a significant gap.

Why it matters for runners specifically:
- Running creates muscle damage that requires protein to repair
- Connective tissue (tendons, ligaments) rebuilds from protein
- Immune function depends on adequate protein intake
- Low protein = longer recovery times, higher injury risk

Practical way to hit your target: aim for 30-40g per meal, 3-4 times per day. Stop treating protein as an afterthought.

High-yield sources: chicken, turkey, salmon, eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, lean beef, protein powder when needed.

DAY 9 — June 11 — Lifestyle
POST TITLE: How to Train When Life Gets in the Way

POST TEXT:
Life will get in the way. That's not a possibility — it's a guarantee.

Work gets crazy. Kids get sick. Travel happens. You have a bad week mentally. Something breaks.

The athletes who make consistent long-term progress aren't the ones who never miss a session — they're the ones who have a system for what to do when they do.

Three rules I follow:

1. Something is always better than nothing. A 20-minute run when you planned 45 still counts. It keeps the habit alive. Do not skip because you can't do the full session.

2. Never miss twice in a row. One missed day is life. Two consecutive missed days starts a pattern. Draw the line there.

3. Reduce, don't cancel. If you're exhausted or overwhelmed, shorten the session — don't eliminate it. 15 easy minutes is worth more than zero.

Consistency over months matters more than perfection over any single week.

DAY 12 — June 14 — Recovery
POST TITLE: Why Your Recovery Days Are Actually Training Days

POST TEXT:
Your body doesn't adapt during training. It adapts during recovery.

The training session is a stimulus. It breaks down muscle tissue, depletes glycogen, creates stress on connective tissue. The adaptation — the getting stronger, faster, more efficient — happens when you rest.

If your recovery days are filled with intense cross-training, extra mileage, or "I'll just do a quick hard run," you're blocking the process your training created.

Recovery day does not mean do nothing (unless you need it). It means:
- Easy walking
- Light mobility work
- Foam rolling
- Extra sleep
- Intentional fueling

Your hard days should be hard. Your easy days should be genuinely easy. The contrast is the point.

DAY 14 — June 16 — Training
POST TITLE: The 3 Run Types Every Athlete Needs In Their Week

POST TEXT:
Not all runs are created equal. And most recreational runners only do one type.

The three runs that should be in every training week:

1. Easy runs (60-75% of your weekly mileage)
Conversational effort. The aerobic foundation everything else is built on. These should feel almost boring. If they feel hard, slow down.

2. Long run (20-30% of your weekly mileage)
Extended time on feet at easy effort. Develops fat oxidation, trains your fueling system, builds the aerobic base for race day demands.

3. Quality sessions — one per week maximum in early training
Tempos, intervals, strides. Structured intensity with a specific purpose. These are not "run fast and see what happens."

If you're only doing one kind of run — and most people are running everything at the same medium-hard effort — you're training in a gray zone that's too hard for recovery and too easy for real performance development.

DAY 15 — June 17 — Nutrition
POST TITLE: Carbs Are Not the Enemy (Especially If You Run)

POST TEXT:
This might be the most important nutrition post I write this month.

Carbohydrates are your primary fuel source for running. Full stop.

During moderate to high-intensity effort, your body runs almost entirely on glycogen — the stored form of carbohydrates. Fat metabolism is too slow to keep up with the energy demands of anything above a light jog.

When you restrict carbohydrates as a runner:
- Training feels harder than it should
- You fatigue earlier
- Recovery takes longer
- Mood and motivation drop
- You're more likely to get sick or injured

The low-carb trend is completely at odds with endurance performance for most athletes.

45-55% of your daily calories should come from carbohydrates if you're training consistently. That is not excessive. That is fuel.

DAY 16 — June 18 — Lifestyle
POST TITLE: Morning vs. Evening Training — Which Is Actually Better?

POST TEXT:
I get this question a lot so let's settle it.

The research on morning vs. evening training shows that performance is slightly better in the evening — body temperature is higher, reaction time is faster, strength output tends to be a bit higher.

But here's what the research also shows: the best time to train is the time you will actually train consistently.

For me, that's morning. If I don't train in the morning, the day happens and it doesn't happen. That's just reality.

Morning training trade-offs: slightly stiffer, more warm-up needed, may need to eat before if sessions are over 60 min.

Evening training trade-offs: generally better performance, but harder to protect the time, harder to fall asleep after intense sessions for some people.

My recommendation: pick the time that fits your life and protect it like a meeting you can't reschedule.

When do you train, and does that time actually work for you?

DAY 17 — June 19 — Training
POST TITLE: Strides: The 10-Minute Add-On That Changes Your Running

POST TEXT:
If you're not doing strides, start doing strides.

Strides are short, controlled accelerations — about 20-30 seconds at roughly 90% effort, with full recovery between. That's it. You add 4-6 of them to the end of an easy run once or twice a week.

Why they work:
- Develop neuromuscular coordination and running efficiency
- Improve stride length and turnover without significant aerobic stress
- Teach your body what controlled fast running feels like
- Add a small performance stimulus without meaningful fatigue

They take 10 extra minutes. They make a meaningful difference in how your easy running feels and in your race day performance.

How to do them: finish your easy run, then do 4-6 x 20 seconds at a fast but controlled effort. Not a sprint. Smooth, relaxed, quick. Walk or jog 60-90 seconds between each one.

If you haven't tried these, add them to your next easy run and let me know how it goes.

DAY 18 — June 20 — Recovery
POST TITLE: The Stress-Training Equation Nobody Talks About

POST TEXT:
Your body doesn't distinguish between physical stress and psychological stress.

Cortisol — your primary stress hormone — is elevated by both hard training and hard life circumstances. And your total stress capacity is a fixed resource.

This means: a week with high work stress, poor sleep, relationship tension, and financial pressure is not the same as a normal week — even if your training plan says it is.

Training load that would be perfectly manageable in a calm week may be too much during a high-stress week. And pushing through anyway is a reliable path to injury, burnout, or illness.

What this means practically:
- Track your life stress, not just your training stress
- On high-stress weeks, reduce volume and keep effort easy
- Sleep becomes even more important as life stress increases
- Recovery is not optional when your life is demanding

I adjust my training every week based on how my life actually looks. That's not weakness — that's intelligent programming.

How does your life stress right now affect your training decisions?

DAY 20 — June 22 — Mindset
POST TITLE: Stop Waiting Until You're Ready

POST TEXT:
Here's the hard truth: you will never feel completely ready to start.

There will always be a reason to wait. The timing isn't right. Life is too busy. You want to lose a few pounds first. You want to feel more motivated. You want to wait until after the next thing.

And meanwhile, months pass.

The athletes I've seen make the fastest progress are not the ones who waited until they felt ready. They're the ones who decided to start before they felt ready — and built readiness through action, not through waiting.

DAY 21 — June 23 — Training
POST TITLE: Injury Prevention: The Work You Do Before the Problem

POST TEXT:
The best injury prevention isn't treatment — it's what you do consistently before anything hurts.

The three most common running injuries I see in returning athletes:

1. IT band syndrome — almost always from ramping mileage too fast combined with weak hip abductors
2. Plantar fasciitis — combination of increased load, tight calves, poor foot strength
3. Shin splints — too much too soon, often from running on hard surfaces with inadequate adaptation time

What prevents all three:

- Ramp up mileage gradually. The 10% rule (increase volume no more than 10% per week) is conservative for a reason.
- Do the hip stability work. Glute bridges, clamshells, lateral band walks. Every week.
- Build calf strength. Calf raises — eccentric especially. Single-leg, full range of motion.
- Take your easy days actually easy. Most overuse injuries accumulate from chronic low-grade overeffort.

If something starts to ache, address it at the first sign — not when it becomes a real problem. The cost of a week of precaution is much lower than the cost of 6-8 weeks of forced rest.

DAY 22 — June 24 — Nutrition
POST TITLE: What Race Day Nutrition Actually Looks Like

POST TEXT:
Race day nutrition is something most people figure out the hard way.

Here's what's worked for me and the athletes I train:

The night before: Normal meal. Higher carb than usual. Nothing new. Nothing experimental. Early enough that you're not going to bed stuffed.

Race morning (for a half or full marathon): Wake up 3 hours before start if possible. 200-300 calories of easily digestible carbs. Low fiber, low fat. Nothing that's going to cause GI issues. Toast and peanut butter, oatmeal, a banana and a bagel. Coffee if that's your normal routine.

During the race: For anything over 60-75 minutes, you need to be taking in carbohydrates. 30-60g per hour. Gels, chews, sports drinks. Practice this in training first — never try a new fuel product on race day.

Post-race: Protein and carbohydrates within 45-60 minutes. Your body needs both after sustained effort.

The biggest mistakes: not fueling at all during the race (bonking), eating something unfamiliar, waiting too long after the race to eat.

DAY 24 — June 26 — Training
POST TITLE: Heart Rate Training: What Zone 2 Actually Is and Why It Matters

POST TEXT:
Zone 2 is one of the most talked about — and most misunderstood — concepts in endurance training.

Here's what it actually is:

Zone 2 is the intensity at which you can sustain a conversation. Aerobic, comfortable, sustainable. It's the intensity at which your body primarily uses fat as a fuel source and builds mitochondrial density — the foundation of all endurance performance.

What Zone 2 is NOT: the effort level that feels like you're working hard enough to count.

Most recreational athletes train at Zone 3 the majority of the time — too hard for aerobic development, not hard enough for performance adaptation. They're stuck in the gray zone.

How to find your Zone 2: talk test (full sentences, no laboring) or heart rate (roughly 60-70% of max heart rate for most people). If you wear a heart rate monitor, see what your HR is on your easy days — if it's consistently above 75%, you're going too hard.

The athletes who spend the most time in Zone 2 build the biggest aerobic engine. That's the whole game.

DAY 26 — June 28 — Recovery
POST TITLE: The Deload Week: Why Backing Off Makes You Better

POST TEXT:
Every fourth week in my training blocks, I cut volume by 30-40%.

Fewer miles. Same schedule. Lower effort ceiling. It feels like I'm going backwards.

I'm not.

Deload weeks exist because your body needs time to fully consolidate the adaptations from the previous three weeks of training. If you keep adding load week over week without a reduction, you accumulate fatigue faster than you accumulate fitness — and eventually something breaks down.

Signs you need a deload:
- Legs feel heavy on easy runs
- Motivation is unusually low
- Sleep quality is declining
- Resting heart rate is elevated
- Niggling soreness that isn't clearing

Signs a deload is working:
- You feel better by day 3
- Easy runs feel easy again by end of week
- You're eager to train again

Don't confuse a deload with being lazy. It's structured programming. Week 4 being easier is the plan, not a deviation from it.

DAY 28 — June 30 — Lifestyle
POST TITLE: Hydration: The Simple Version That Actually Works

POST TEXT:
Hydration does not need to be complicated. Here's the version that actually works:

Drink water consistently throughout the day — not in large quantities all at once. Spacing it out is more effective than chugging.

Check your urine color. Pale yellow is the target. Dark yellow means you're behind. Clear means you've over-hydrated (less common but not ideal either).

On training days over 60 minutes, add electrolytes. Water alone isn't enough for extended effort — sodium, potassium, and magnesium matter for muscle function and preventing cramps.

The hydration mistake I see most: athletes who drink almost nothing during the workday and then try to compensate by drinking a lot in the evening. You can't front-load hydration. It doesn't work that way.

Simple system:
- Glass of water first thing in the morning before anything else
- Water with every meal
- Set a reminder every 60-90 minutes during your workday if you're inconsistent
- Electrolytes on longer training days

That's it. No app required.
