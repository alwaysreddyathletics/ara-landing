How to Run a
Sub 3:30 Marathon

By Coach Tyler Reddy · Always Reddy Athletics · May 2026 · 8 min read

In December 2025 I ran the Honolulu Marathon at a 10:09/mi pace. Five months later I ran the Pittsburgh Marathon in 3:27:32 — a 7:55/mi average on a harder course.

That's a 1:18 improvement in under a year, on the same distance, with the same body. No magic supplement, no secret trick. Just a structured plan, consistent execution, and a few key things I changed.

This article breaks down exactly how I did it — and what it takes for most runners to break 3:30.

What Sub 3:30 Actually Requires

A 3:30 marathon is an 8:01/mi average for 26.2 miles. That's it. The question is how you train to sustain that pace when miles 20–26 are trying to break you.

Most runners who miss 3:30 don't fail because they're not fit enough. They fail because:

  • They go out too fast — aiming for 3:25 and blowing up at mile 18
  • Their long runs are too slow — never training at or near race pace
  • They skip strength work — form collapses late in the race when it matters most
  • They don't taper properly — showing up tired or underprepared for race week

Fix those four things and you're most of the way there.

The Weekly Training Structure

To break 3:30 you need to be running 4 days per week with purpose. Not just easy miles — structured sessions with specific goals.

The 4 Run Types

1. The Easy Run

2–3 per week. Conversational pace — you should be able to talk in full sentences. Most runners run their easy runs too fast. Slow down. These runs build aerobic base and speed your recovery between hard sessions.

2. The Tempo Run

Once per week. Comfortably hard — around 30–40 seconds per mile faster than your goal marathon pace. Typically 20–40 minutes at tempo effort. This is the single most important workout for marathon fitness.

3. The Interval Session

Once per week. Mile repeats, 800s, or 1200s at 5K–10K effort. These build your VO2 max and teach your legs what real speed feels like. They also make 8:01/mi feel easy by comparison.

4. The Long Run

Once per week. The cornerstone of marathon training. Build to 20–22 miles over the training cycle. The last 4–6 miles of your longest runs should include some miles at marathon pace — not just shuffling through.

The Training Blocks

A 15-week plan for sub 3:30 breaks into three distinct phases:

Weeks 1–5: Base Building

Easy miles, one tempo run per week, long run building from 12 to 16 miles. The goal here is aerobic base and getting your legs used to consistent volume. Don't chase pace in this phase — it's not the point yet.

Weeks 6–11: Race-Specific Work

This is where the real work happens. Interval sessions come in, tempo runs get longer, and your long run starts incorporating race pace miles. By week 10 you should be running 4–5 miles at marathon pace within your long run.

Weeks 12–15: Peak and Taper

Peak long run (20–22 miles) in week 12, then a structured taper. Most runners get the taper wrong — they either cut too much too fast or stay at full volume. The goal is to arrive at the start line fresh but sharp.

The thing most people miss: strength work. Two short strength sessions per week — especially in weeks 1–6 — made the single biggest difference in my Pittsburgh build. Strong glutes and hips mean your form holds together in miles 20–26 when everything else is falling apart.

Race Day Execution

For 3:30, your splits should look like this:

  • Miles 1–6: 8:05–8:10/mi. Deliberately conservative. The crowd and adrenaline will push you faster — resist it.
  • Miles 7–18: 8:00–8:05/mi. Settle into race pace. You should feel like you're holding back slightly.
  • Miles 19–22: Hold pace. This is where most runners start slipping. Focus on form — arms, cadence, breathing.
  • Miles 23–26.2: Leave everything you have. If you executed the first 22 miles correctly, you have something left.

Nutrition and Hydration

Simple rules that cover 90% of what you need:

  • Take a gel every 45 minutes starting at mile 4–5, not when you feel like you need one
  • Drink at every aid station — even a few sips
  • Race in shoes you've trained in for at least 3–4 long runs
  • Nothing new on race day — no new food, no new gear

The Bottom Line

Breaking 3:30 is achievable for most runners who are willing to train consistently for 15 weeks. The plan isn't complicated — but it has to be followed. Four runs per week, structured sessions, strength work, and a disciplined race day execution.
