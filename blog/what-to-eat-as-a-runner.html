What to Eat as a Runner — Fuel That Actually Works

By Coach Tyler Reddy · Always Reddy Athletics · June 2026 · 8 min read

Runner nutrition is one of the most over-complicated topics in endurance sports. There are fad diets telling you to go low-carb, supplements promising to transform your race times, and influencers eating nothing but açaí bowls and calling it a training strategy. Most of it is noise.

The honest answer to what to eat as a runner is a lot less exciting than the internet would have you believe — and a lot more effective.

When I was building from a 4:46 marathon to a 3:27, I didn't follow a trendy diet. I didn't eliminate food groups or buy expensive powders. I ate real food, at the right times, in the right amounts. That's it. And I felt better, trained harder, and recovered faster than I ever had.

Here's what actually matters.

Stop Under-Eating. Seriously.

The most common nutrition mistake I see runners make isn't eating the wrong things. It's not eating enough. Runners burn significant calories — and when you're training consistently, your body needs fuel to adapt. Chronic under-eating leads to fatigue, slower recovery, hormonal disruption, and eventually injury.

This is especially true for working adults who are busy during the day and lose track of their intake. You grab a coffee in the morning, skip lunch because of a meeting, eat a small dinner, and wonder why your long run on Saturday felt terrible. You went into it depleted.

Fueling for training isn't the same as eating for weight loss. If you're in a serious training block, this is not the time to be in a significant calorie deficit. You can work on body composition in the off-season. During a training block, your job is to fuel performance and recovery.

The Three Nutrients Runners Actually Need to Nail

Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are your body's preferred fuel for running — period. Low-carb running is a real thing but it's optimized for ultra-distance fat adaptation, not for recreational athletes trying to PR a marathon. If you want to run fast, you need glycogen, and glycogen comes from carbs.

The key is timing. Prioritize carbs before and after hard sessions. On easy days, you don't need to go overboard. Before long runs and quality sessions, eat something substantial — oatmeal with fruit and honey, a bagel with peanut butter, rice with eggs. Don't show up to a hard workout running on empty.

Protein

Protein is how your body rebuilds muscle after you break it down in training. Most runners don't eat enough of it. The target for training athletes is 0.7–1 gram per pound of bodyweight per day — more than the generic "0.8 grams per kilogram" advice you'll see on basic health sites.

Spread it across meals. Your body can only absorb and use so much protein at once — around 30–40 grams per sitting. A 200-gram chicken breast at dinner isn't going to cover your whole day's needs. Build protein into every meal: eggs or Greek yogurt at breakfast, chicken or tuna at lunch, fish or meat at dinner, cottage cheese or a protein shake as a snack.

Fats

Don't fear fat. Healthy fats from avocado, olive oil, nuts, fatty fish, and eggs support hormone function, reduce inflammation, and provide long-duration energy. They're not the enemy. What doesn't help is excess processed fat from fast food consumed out of convenience because you didn't meal prep. That's a lifestyle issue, not a macronutrient issue.

Meal Timing Matters More Than Most People Think

When you eat is almost as important as what you eat, especially around training sessions.

Before a Run

For easy runs under 60 minutes, you don't need much — a banana or a small snack is fine, or nothing at all if you're going first thing in the morning. For hard sessions or long runs, eat a proper meal 2–3 hours before: something carb-heavy, moderate protein, low fat and fiber (fat and fiber slow digestion and can cause GI issues mid-run). Oatmeal, toast with eggs, or a bagel with light cream cheese are all solid.

After a Run

The 30-minute post-workout window is real. After a hard session or long run, your muscles are primed to uptake nutrients for recovery. Hit this window with a protein + carb combination: chocolate milk, Greek yogurt with fruit, a protein shake with a banana, eggs on toast. Don't wait until you shower, scroll your phone, and eat two hours later. Your recovery starts the moment you stop running.

During Long Runs

Once your long runs exceed 75–90 minutes, you need to fuel during the run. This is non-negotiable for marathon training. Practice taking in 30–60 grams of carbohydrates per hour on your long runs — gels, chews, sports drinks, or real food like dates and banana slices. Whatever you plan to use on race day, train with it. Race day is not the time to experiment.

Sample Long Run Fueling (2.5 hr run)

Pre-run: oatmeal with banana + honey, 2 hrs before. During: gel or chews at 45 min, 90 min, and 2 hrs (30–45g carbs each). Water or electrolyte drink throughout. Post-run within 30 min: protein shake + fruit or chocolate milk.

Hydration Is the Thing Everyone Gets Wrong

Dehydration doesn't just feel bad — it directly degrades performance. Even 2% dehydration measurably slows you down. And the problem isn't usually the run itself. It's the 20 hours before the run where you're sitting at a desk drinking coffee and forgetting to drink water.

Aim for half your bodyweight in ounces of water per day as a baseline — more on training days and hot weather. Add electrolytes (sodium, potassium, magnesium) on long run days and in warm conditions. Plain water alone doesn't cut it when you're sweating heavily. A pinch of sea salt in your water bottle or an electrolyte tablet does the job without needing a $40 supplement.

What a Good Eating Day Actually Looks Like

Theory is nice. Here's what it looks like in practice on a quality session day:

  • 6:30am: Wake up, drink 16 oz water
  • 7:00am: Breakfast — 3 eggs scrambled, 2 slices sourdough toast, half avocado, fruit. Coffee.
  • 12:00pm: Lunch — large salad with grilled chicken, quinoa, olive oil dressing, handful of nuts
  • 3:30pm: Pre-workout snack — banana + tablespoon of almond butter
  • 5:00pm: Interval session
  • 5:55pm: Post-run — Greek yogurt with berries and granola (within 30 min)
  • 7:30pm: Dinner — salmon, roasted sweet potato, steamed broccoli with olive oil
  • 9:00pm: Optional — cottage cheese or casein protein if hungry (slow-digesting, great before sleep)

That's real food, timed strategically, hitting protein targets across the day. Nothing exotic. No supplements except maybe some electrolytes and a basic vitamin D. This is the kind of eating that built the training that ran a 3:27 marathon.

The Lifestyle Side of Runner Nutrition

Here's what nobody tells you: good nutrition habits and good life habits overlap almost completely. The person who eats well, sleeps enough, manages stress, and trains consistently is living the athletic lifestyle — not executing a diet protocol.

Meal prep on Sundays so you're not making bad choices at noon on a Tuesday because there's nothing in the fridge. Keep fruit and nuts accessible. Cook protein in bulk — grilled chicken, hard-boiled eggs, ground turkey — so there's always something ready. Don't rely on willpower at 7pm when you're tired and hungry. Make the right choice the easy choice by setting up your environment correctly.

The best diet for a runner is one you can sustain. If your current eating approach requires constant vigilance and makes social eating miserable, it's not sustainable. Eat real food, hit your protein, time your carbs around training, and stop overthinking the rest.
