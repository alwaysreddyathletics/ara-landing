Marathon Training While Working Full Time

By Coach Tyler Reddy · Always Reddy Athletics · June 2026 · 8 min read

In December 2025 I ran the Honolulu Marathon at a 10:09/mi pace. Five months later I ran the Pittsburgh Marathon in 3:27:32 — a 7:55/mi average on a harder course. That's a 1:18 improvement in under a year, on the same distance, with the same body. No magic supplement, no secret trick.

And I did it while working full time.

I'm not telling you that to flex. I'm telling you because the number one thing I hear from people who want to run a marathon is some version of: "I just don't have the time." And I get it — the image of marathon training looks like elite athletes logging 80-mile weeks in the morning, napping in the afternoon, and doing nothing else. That's not your life. It's not mine either.

But here's what's actually true: marathon training while working full time is absolutely doable — if you're willing to be honest about your constraints and build a system around them instead of fighting them.

First, Reframe What "Enough" Training Looks Like

Most recreational marathon plans are written for people with unlimited time and unlimited recovery capacity. They assume you can run 5–6 days per week, add a long run every weekend, and stack quality sessions back to back. For working adults — especially those with families, commutes, and jobs that drain mental energy — that schedule is a recipe for burnout or injury.

The good news: you don't need 5 days of running to run a strong marathon. Four quality days per week, structured correctly, is enough. What kills working athletes isn't low mileage — it's junk mileage. Running extra days at medium-hard efforts that aren't easy enough to recover from and aren't hard enough to create adaptation. Cut those out. Every session should have a purpose.

How to Actually Build the Schedule

Forget fitting training around your work calendar. Build it the other way: decide when your non-negotiable runs are, put them in your calendar like a meeting, and protect them.

Here's what works for most working athletes:

  • Tuesday: Quality session (tempo, intervals, or race-pace work) — 45–60 min total. Do this before work or at lunch. It has a defined start and end. Easy to protect.
  • Thursday: Easy run — 45–55 min. Conversational pace. This is your recovery run. Don't make it hard.
  • Saturday: Long run — 90 min to 2:30 depending on your cycle. This is the cornerstone of marathon training. Guard it.
  • Sunday or Monday: Easy shakeout — 30–40 min. Optional but valuable. If life happens, skip it without guilt.

That's it. Four days, clear purposes, enough recovery built in to actually adapt.

Sample Quality Session (Tuesday)

Warm up 15 min easy. Main set: 4 x 1 mile at goal marathon pace + 30 sec faster, with 90-sec jog recovery between. Cool down 10 min easy. Total: ~55 min. Hard enough to build fitness. Short enough to fit in a lunch break.

The Long Run Is Non-Negotiable

Everything else in your training week is negotiable to a point. The long run is not. Miss a Tuesday interval session — fine, you'll survive. Miss three long runs in a row and you're showing up to race day unprepared for the distance. The long run builds the aerobic infrastructure that carries you through miles 18–26.

Saturday mornings work for most people because they're the one time in the week that belongs entirely to you before the weekend pulls you in twelve directions. Set it as a habit. Tell your family, your partner, whoever needs to know: Saturday mornings are mine from 6 to 9.

And don't run it too hard. Long runs should be easy — 60 to 90 seconds per mile slower than your goal race pace. The point is time on feet and aerobic adaptation, not suffering. If you're grinding through long runs at medium-hard effort, you're accumulating fatigue you can't recover from in a working week.

Energy Management Is the Real Constraint

Time is the obvious constraint for working athletes. But the real one is energy. After a draining day of meetings, decisions, and screen time, going home and running hard is genuinely difficult. Your nervous system is taxed even if your legs feel fine. This matters because it affects how much you can actually get out of a hard session.

Stack your hard workouts on your lowest-stress days. For most people, Tuesday is better than Friday. Monday morning after a weekend of rest is often better than Thursday afternoon after a brutal work week. Pay attention to when you feel sharpest, and build your quality sessions around that.

This is also why the easy runs need to be genuinely easy. You're not recovering from yesterday's workout in isolation — you're recovering from your entire life. Easy days are when your body catches up. Running them at medium effort steals recovery without building much fitness. It's the mistake most working athletes make on repeat.

Nutrition and Sleep Are Part of the Training Plan

You can't out-train a chronic sleep deficit. Full stop. When I was building toward Pittsburgh, I treated 7.5 hours of sleep as a training variable — not a nice-to-have, but part of the plan. If you're staying up late and waking up early to squeeze in a run, you're borrowing from your recovery to fund your training, and that debt comes due on race day.

Same goes for nutrition. Working adults often under-eat during the day — busy schedules, skipped lunches, coffee standing in for calories — then come home depleted and try to train. That pattern fails eventually. Here's what actually works:

  • Eat a real breakfast with protein before morning runs
  • Don't skip lunch on training days, even when work is crazy
  • Have a protein + carb snack within 30 minutes of finishing a hard session
  • Front-load your calories earlier in the day so you're not running on fumes at 6pm

How to Handle the Weeks When Everything Falls Apart

They will happen. The work trip. The sick kid. The stretch of 60-hour weeks. You will miss runs. This is inevitable and not a problem — unless you respond to it by trying to "make up" missed training by cramming two sessions into one day or skipping your rest day. Don't do that. Missed training is just missed. Absorb it and move forward.

The athletes who run strong marathons while working full time are not the ones who never miss a workout. They're the ones who stay consistent over months, execute the key sessions when it matters, and don't let a bad week become a bad month.

Consistency over months beats perfection over weeks. A 16-week training block where you hit 85% of your sessions is better than a 16-week block where you nail the first six weeks and then blow up trying to make up for missed work.

The Identity Shift That Makes This Work Long-Term

The athletes I see make the biggest improvements aren't the ones who are most talented or have the most free time. They're the ones who've fully committed to the identity of being an athlete — not someone who "tries to fit in some running," but someone who trains, and who structures their life accordingly.

That shift is subtle but it changes everything. When you're "someone who trains," the 6am alarm isn't a battle — it's just what you do. The Saturday long run isn't an imposition on your weekend — it's the anchor. The dietary choices aren't restriction — they're just how you eat now.

You can work full time and train seriously for a marathon. Thousands of people do it every season. The ones who succeed don't have more hours in the day. They've just decided that training is part of their life, not a thing they do when everything else is handled.
