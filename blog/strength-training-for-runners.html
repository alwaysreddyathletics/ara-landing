Strength Training
for Runners

By Coach Tyler Reddy · Always Reddy Athletics · August 2026 · 7 min read

Most runners either skip strength training entirely or do it wrong — random exercises, no progression, whatever a workout app suggested that day. Both are a mistake. Strength training is one of the highest-leverage things a runner can do: it builds the resilience that keeps you off the injured list, and it builds the power that makes you faster on tired legs late in a race.

This isn't a bodybuilding program bolted onto your running. It's a specific approach built for people whose primary sport is running — enough to matter, not so much that it wrecks your legs for tomorrow's run.

Who this is for: Runners training for anything from a 5K to a marathon who currently do little or no strength work, or who lift without a clear plan. You don't need a gym background — you need 2–3 sessions a week and a program that respects your mileage.

Why Runners Skip It — and Why That's a Mistake

The two excuses I hear most: "I don't want to get bulky" and "I don't have time." Neither holds up.

Strength training two to three times a week, built around compound lifts, does not add meaningful bulk to a runner's frame — it takes a dedicated hypertrophy program and a calorie surplus to do that, and that's not what this is. What it does add is tendon and connective tissue strength, which is what actually breaks down in overuse injuries like runner's knee, shin splints, and IT band syndrome. Stronger tissue tolerates more mileage before it complains.

And it doesn't take long. Two 30–40 minute sessions a week is enough to move the needle. The runners who "don't have time" for strength work usually have time — they just haven't decided it's worth prioritizing over a fourth easy run that isn't doing much for them anyway.

What Actually Moves the Needle

Five movement patterns cover almost everything a runner needs:

Hinge

Deadlifts, RDLs, single-leg RDLs. Builds the posterior chain — hamstrings and glutes — that drives you forward and protects your lower back on long runs.

Squat

Goblet squats, split squats, step-ups. Builds quad and glute strength for hills, and single-leg variations build the stability that keeps your knees tracking correctly mile after mile.

Push & Pull

Push-ups, overhead press, rows. Running is a lower-body sport, but upper body and core strength hold your form together when you're fatigued in the back half of a race — that's when form breaks down and pace falls apart.

Carry

Farmer's carries, suitcase carries. Deceptively simple, and one of the best exercises for the core and grip stability that a lot of "ab work" never actually builds.

How Often, and When

Two to three sessions a week is the sweet spot for most runners. More than that starts competing with your running for recovery.

Day Focus
After an easy run Best pairing — legs are already warm, and the run was low-intensity enough that a lift afterward won't compound fatigue
Rest day Good alternative if you'd rather keep lift days fully separate from running days
Before a hard run Avoid — you want fresh legs for intervals, tempo work, or long runs, not legs already fatigued from squats

A Sample Week

  • Monday: Easy run + lift (squat pattern, push, carry)
  • Tuesday: Interval or tempo run — legs fresh, no lifting
  • Wednesday: Easy run + lift (hinge pattern, pull, core)
  • Thursday: Rest or easy cross-training
  • Friday: Easy run
  • Saturday: Long run — legs fresh, no lifting
  • Sunday: Rest or optional light mobility work

Common Mistakes

  • Going too heavy, too soon. Progressive overload works — jumping straight to max effort doesn't. Add weight or reps gradually over weeks, not sessions.
  • Skipping single-leg work. Running is a single-leg sport. Bilateral squats and deadlifts are useful, but single-leg variations build the specific stability that prevents injury.
  • Treating it as optional during race prep. This is when most runners drop strength work to "save energy" — exactly backwards. Maintenance-level lifting through a training block keeps you resilient when volume is highest and injury risk is greatest.
  • No progression plan. Random exercises with no structure produce random results. A program that builds week over week is what actually makes you stronger.

The bottom line: strength training isn't extra credit for runners — it's part of the training. Two focused sessions a week, built around the right movement patterns, done consistently, will do more for your durability and your finishing speed than almost any other change you could make.

Where to Start

If you're new to lifting, start with a beginner program that teaches the movement patterns with dumbbells before adding load. If you've got a base already, a structured intermediate program with real progressive overload will do more than winging it in the gym ever will.
