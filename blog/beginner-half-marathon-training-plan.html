Beginner Half Marathon
Training Plan

By Coach Tyler Reddy · Always Reddy Athletics · May 2026 · 9 min read

13.1 miles is a serious distance. It will test your aerobic base, your patience, and your ability to keep moving when your legs are begging you to stop. But it's also completely doable for almost anyone who's willing to train consistently for 10 weeks.

This guide walks you through everything: what a realistic beginner training schedule looks like, how to structure your long runs, what to do when you miss a session, and how to actually cross the finish line feeling strong — not destroyed.

Who this is for: You can run 3–4 miles without stopping, you've been running casually for at least a month, and you've signed up for your first half marathon (or you're seriously considering it). You don't need prior race experience.

The Core Principles

Before the schedule, understand why it's built the way it is:

Three Runs Per Week Is Enough

Most beginner plans try to do too much. Three quality runs per week — one easy, one moderate, one long — is enough to get you to the finish line. More than that and you're just adding injury risk without proportionate benefit.

The Long Run Is the Priority

Every other run exists to support the long run. The long run is where your body learns to go the distance. Don't skip it. Don't cut it short. If you can only fit in two runs in a week, make one of them the long run.

Slow Down

Your long runs should be conversational — you should be able to speak in full sentences without gasping. Most beginners run their easy runs too fast, accumulate fatigue, and end up injured by week 6. Run slower than feels necessary. You'll thank yourself later.

Build In Recovery

The plan below includes deload weeks — weeks where mileage drops intentionally. This isn't weakness. This is how you absorb the training and arrive at the start line healthy.

What "Easy" Actually Means

Every run marked "easy" should be run at a truly easy effort. A simple test: if you can't hold a conversation, you're going too fast. Slow down until you can.

Easy pace for most beginners is 60–90 seconds per mile slower than they think they should be running. That's fine. The easy runs aren't about speed — they're about building aerobic base and recovering between long runs.

What to Do When You Miss a Session

You will miss sessions. Here's how to handle it:

  • Miss a short run: Skip it and move on. Don't try to make it up — just continue with the schedule.
  • Miss the long run: Do it the next day if possible. The long run is the one session worth rescheduling.
  • Miss a full week: Drop back one week in the plan and continue from there. Don't try to jump ahead.
  • Sick or injured: Rest. No run is worth making an injury worse. Return when you feel 100%.

Race Day Strategy

Your only job on race day is to not go out too fast. Here's how:

  • Start in the back half of your corral. The adrenaline will make mile 1 feel easy. That feeling is a trap.
  • Run miles 1–4 at your slowest comfortable pace. Let the faster runners go. You'll see many of them again around mile 10.
  • Miles 5–10 are your cruise miles. Find a rhythm and stay there.
  • Miles 11–13.1: You've done the distance in training. Trust it. Keep moving forward.
  • Walk through aid stations if you need to — it's faster than trying to run and drink simultaneously.

The one thing that will make or break your race: pacing. Run the first mile slower than feels right. Seriously. Almost every half marathon blowup starts in miles 1–3.

Gear You Actually Need

You don't need much. The essentials:

  • Running shoes that fit well and have at least 50–60 miles on them before race day (broken in but not worn out)
  • Moisture-wicking socks — cotton socks are a blister factory over 10+ miles
  • Body Glide or Vaseline — anywhere you chafe on shorter runs will chafe badly at 13 miles
  • 2–3 gels — take one around mile 4, one around mile 8, one in your pocket for miles 10+
