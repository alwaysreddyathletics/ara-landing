How to Build a Morning Routine as an Athlete

By Coach Tyler Reddy · Always Reddy Athletics · June 2026 · 7 min read

The internet's version of an athlete's morning routine is exhausting. Cold plunge at 4:30am, 45-minute meditation, journaling three pages of gratitude, red light therapy, 11 supplements, a celery juice, and a PR in your workout — all before 7am. If you need a six-figure brand deal to afford your morning routine, it's not a routine. It's content.

Here's the real version: a morning routine for an athlete is any consistent set of habits that sets you up to train well, think clearly, and live sustainably. That's it. The specifics are yours to figure out, but the principles apply across the board.

When I was training from December to May — the stretch where I took 1:18 off my marathon time — my mornings weren't glamorous. They were consistent. That consistency is what made the difference.

Start With Why Your Morning Matters

Most people think about morning routines backwards. They try to build the perfect morning and then figure out why they're doing it. Flip that. Ask yourself: what do I need to be true at 8am for training and life to both go well?

For a working athlete, the answer usually involves some combination of: having trained or knowing when you'll train, having eaten something real, having some mental clarity before the day pulls you in every direction. Your morning routine is the scaffolding that makes those things happen consistently — even when motivation is low, even when work is chaotic, even when it's cold and dark outside.

The goal is not an impressive routine. The goal is a functional one.

The One Rule That Actually Matters: Protect Your Sleep

I'm going to say something that contradicts 80% of the "athlete morning routine" content out there: don't sacrifice sleep for an early wake-up time.

Seven to nine hours of sleep is the most powerful performance lever available to you. More than any supplement, any training hack, any early morning ritual. If you're cutting your sleep from 7.5 hours to 6 hours to get up at 5am, you're trading the thing that matters most for an earlier start time. That's a bad trade.

Your wake-up time should be determined by your bedtime, not the other way around. If you want to wake up at 5:30am, that means lights out by 10pm — not scrolling until midnight and setting an aggressive alarm. Work backwards from the sleep you need.

During my Pittsburgh training block, I prioritized 7.5 hours every night. I went to bed earlier instead of waking up earlier. That single change improved my recovery measurably — I felt better in hard sessions, my resting heart rate dropped, and my energy was more stable throughout the day.

A Functional Athlete Morning — No Cold Plunge Required

Here's what a solid athlete morning actually looks like. This isn't prescriptive — it's a model you can adapt:

The Functional Athlete Morning (60–75 min)

Wake + Hydrate (5 min): Don't check your phone. Drink 16 oz of water before you do anything else. You woke up mildly dehydrated. Fix that first.

Light Movement (10 min): Not a workout — just waking up your body. Hip circles, leg swings, some light mobility work. This is especially important on training days to prime your joints and CNS for the effort ahead.

Eat Something Real (15 min): Protein + carbs within 45 minutes of waking on training days. On rest days, less urgency but still eat. Eggs and toast. Greek yogurt and fruit. Oatmeal with protein powder stirred in. Don't skip this.

The Thing That Grounds You (10–15 min): This is personal. For some people it's a walk. For others it's journaling, reading, sitting with coffee before anyone else wakes up. No phone. No news. Just a few minutes of intentional quiet before the day starts demanding things from you.

Train or Prepare to Train (time varies): If you're running in the morning, this is when it happens. If you're training in the evening, use this time to review what's on your plan and mentally commit to it.

That's it. Hydrate, move a little, eat, find some quiet, train or prepare to train. Sixty to seventy-five minutes. Nothing exotic.

Morning Training vs. Evening Training — Which Is Better?

There's no objectively superior time to train. The best time to run is the time you'll actually do it consistently. That said, there are real tradeoffs worth knowing.

Morning Training

Pros: it's done before work, before decision fatigue sets in, before anything can cancel it. Consistency rates for morning exercisers are higher than evening exercisers — life has fewer opportunities to derail a 6am workout than a 6pm one. Cons: you need to actually sleep enough to support it, and your body temperature and muscle readiness are lower in the early morning, so warm-ups matter more.

Evening Training

Pros: your body is physically primed — core temperature is higher, reaction time is better, and research suggests peak athletic performance tends to happen in the late afternoon or early evening. Cons: higher cancellation risk as the day fills up, and hard evening sessions can interfere with sleep for some people.

If you're genuinely unsure, start with mornings. The consistency benefit outweighs the physiological advantage of evenings for most working athletes.

The Phone Problem

This is where most people's morning routines fall apart before they even start. You wake up, check your phone, and suddenly you're in reactive mode — responding to messages, processing news, scrolling content. Your brain shifts from rested and neutral to overstimulated before you've had a glass of water.

The fix is simple and annoying: don't touch your phone for the first 30–45 minutes of your day. Buy a $10 alarm clock so your phone isn't on your nightstand. Put your morning routine between waking up and the first phone check. You'll find that those undisturbed minutes are the clearest thinking of your entire day.

Build the Routine Around Your Life, Not Someone Else's

The trap with morning routine content is that it makes you think there's a universal optimal sequence. There isn't. A 28-year-old single person with a remote job has completely different constraints than a 38-year-old with two kids and a commute. Both can build excellent athlete mornings — they'll just look different.

Ask these questions about your own life:

  • What time do I actually need to leave the house (or start working)?
  • What time do I need to be asleep to get 7.5 hours if I wake up then?
  • What are the 2–3 things that, if I do them before 9am, make the rest of my day measurably better?
  • What's the simplest version of this I can do even on a hard day?

That last question matters most. The morning routine you actually maintain on a hard Thursday is worth more than the perfect morning you only pull off twice a week. Build for consistency, not for the best-case version of your life.

Your Morning Routine Is a Reflection of Your Identity

The longer I've trained seriously, the more I've come to see morning habits as less about optimization and more about identity. When you have a consistent morning routine as an athlete, you're not just building fitness — you're reinforcing who you are. Every morning you wake up and execute the routine, you're voting for the version of yourself that trains, eats well, and takes their health seriously.

That compound interest adds up. After a few months of consistent mornings, training isn't something you do. It's who you are. And that identity is what sustains you through the hard patches — the weeks when motivation drops, when work gets brutal, when life doesn't cooperate. The athletes who show up anyway aren't more disciplined than you. They've just made showing up part of their identity.

Start smaller than you think you need to. A 20-minute morning routine you do every day is more powerful than a 90-minute routine you do twice a week. Build the habit first. Add to it once it's automatic.
