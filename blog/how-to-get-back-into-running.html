How to Get Back Into Running After Years Off

By Coach Tyler Reddy · Always Reddy Athletics · June 2026 · 7 min read

You used to run. Maybe in high school, maybe in your twenties, maybe just a few years ago before life got in the way. Now you lace up and half a mile in, you're winded, your knees are complaining, and the ego hit is real. You remember being fitter than this. You remember running feeling good.

Here's what I want you to understand before anything else: the fitness you built before isn't completely gone. Your cardiovascular system has a long memory. Your body knows how to run. What's gone is the specific adaptation — the tendons, the connective tissue, the neuromuscular patterns. Those come back fast, but they come back on their own timeline, not yours.

The number one reason people fail when they try to get back into running after years off is that they run too hard, too soon. Their lungs recover faster than their legs, so they feel capable of doing more — and they pay for it with shin splints, knee pain, or a stress fracture that sidelines them for months.

Don't be that person. Let's do this right.

Week 1–2: Slower Than You Think

The first thing I tell every athlete coming back from a long break: if you feel embarrassed by how slow you're going, you're probably at the right pace. Easy running should feel easy. You should be able to hold a full conversation without gasping. If you can't, slow down — or walk.

Walk/run intervals are not a sign of weakness. They're smart programming. A 20-minute outing where you alternate 2 minutes of jogging with 1 minute of walking is more productive than grinding through a 3-mile run that leaves you hobbling for three days. The goal in the first two weeks is to reintroduce your body to the motion and the impact, not to prove anything.

Week 1–2 Sample Session

20–25 min total. Warm up with 5 min brisk walk. Then: 2 min easy jog / 1 min walk, repeat 5–6 times. Cool down with 5 min walk. Do this 3x per week with a full rest day between each session.

Three sessions per week. Not five. Not every day because you're finally motivated. Three. Your body needs the rest days to adapt — that's when the actual progress happens.

The Comeback Trap Most People Fall Into

Week one goes well. You feel good. Week two, you add a fourth day. Maybe a fifth. The runs get longer because you feel capable. Then around week three or four, something hurts. You take a few days off. The pain fades, you go back too hard, and the cycle repeats.

I've seen this pattern dozens of times. It's not a motivation problem. It's a pacing problem — not just in terms of running pace, but in how fast you're ramping up your training load overall.

The 10% rule exists for a reason. Don't increase your weekly mileage by more than 10% from one week to the next. If you ran 10 miles this week, cap it at 11 next week. It feels slow. It works.

Your aerobic engine will adapt faster than your structural tissue. Cartilage, tendons, and ligaments don't have the same blood supply as muscles. They take longer to strengthen. The patience you apply in the first 4–6 weeks determines whether you're still running at month three or sitting on the couch icing your knee.

What Your Body Actually Needs Right Now

Running is the easy part. The support structure around it is what most people skip — and what makes the difference between a comeback that sticks and one that fizzles out by week six.

Sleep

Seven to nine hours is not optional when you're rebuilding fitness. This is when your body repairs micro-tears in muscle, consolidates movement patterns, and regulates the hormones that drive adaptation. If you're cutting sleep to make time for early runs, you're robbing Peter to pay Paul. The run matters less than the recovery from the run.

Protein

Your muscles are doing work they haven't done in years. Feed them. Aim for at least 0.7–1 gram of protein per pound of bodyweight daily, spread across meals. If you're not hitting that consistently, you're leaving recovery on the table.

Easy Days Stay Easy

When you add a second or third run to your week, those extra sessions need to be genuinely easy — not "I could've gone harder" easy, but conversational, boring, almost-too-slow easy. Beginners and returners make every run a medium-hard effort. That's a dead end. Easy runs build aerobic base. Hard efforts build fitness on top of it. You need both, but you need a lot more easy than hard — especially early.

The Identity Piece No One Talks About

Here's something that doesn't show up in training plans: coming back to running after a long break is partly an identity shift. You're not the runner you were. You're a different version of yourself, starting from a different place. The sooner you accept that, the faster you'll actually progress.

In December 2025 I ran the Honolulu Marathon at a 10:09/mi pace. I wasn't proud of the number, but I was honest about where I was. That honesty was the foundation. I didn't train like the athlete I used to be. I trained like the athlete I was becoming. Five months later I ran Pittsburgh in 3:27:32 — a 7:55/mi average on a harder course.

The gap between where you are and where you want to be is just time and consistent work. No shortcut, but also no mystery.

A Simple 4-Week Return Framework

Here's a basic structure to get you from "barely running" to a genuine 3-day-per-week habit with building mileage:

  • Week 1: 3 sessions, 20 min each — walk/run intervals (2 min jog, 1 min walk)
  • Week 2: 3 sessions, 25 min each — 3:1 jog/walk intervals, finishing with a continuous 10-min easy jog
  • Week 3: 3 sessions — two 25-min easy continuous runs + one 30-min run with the last 10 min at a slightly more purposeful effort
  • Week 4: 3–4 sessions, 25–35 min each — all continuous easy running, total weekly mileage around 10–12 miles

By the end of week four, you're not a beginner anymore. You're a runner who's building. From here, the path forward is clear — add mileage gradually, introduce one quality session per week, keep easy days easy, and sleep like it's your job.

Don't skip the boring stuff. The athletes who build lasting fitness show up for the unglamorous miles — the slow easy runs, the extra sleep, the consistent protein. The exciting workouts are maybe 20% of the equation. The other 80% is discipline in the basics.

When to Pick a Goal Race

Once you've built 6–8 consistent weeks, pick a race. It doesn't need to be a marathon. A 5K 12 weeks out or a half marathon 16–20 weeks out gives you something to train toward. Having a date on the calendar changes how you show up for every session. It creates structure, stakes, and momentum.

Don't wait until you "feel ready" to register. Register, then get ready. That's how it works.
